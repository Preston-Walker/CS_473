17.3The big picture

An SVM sorts things into two groups by drawing the widest possible "road" between them.

Say you have examples from two groups: approved and denied loans, spam and real email, tumors that turned out benign or malignant. You want a rule that sorts new examples into the right group. A support vector machine (SVM) is one way to learn that rule. Each of the four subsections adds one idea:

17.3.1
Draw the widest road
Of all the lines that split the groups, pick the one with the most empty space on both sides.
17.3.2
Only the edge cases matter
Rewriting the problem shows that the road depends only on the few points touching its edges, the support vectors.
17.3.3
Allow a few mistakes
Real data is messy. Let some points sit on the road or on the wrong side, and charge a penalty for each one.
17.3.4
Bend the road
Swap every dot product for a kernel and the straight road becomes a curve, at almost no extra cost.

Three things to know before starting

  • Labels are $-1$ and $+1$ (not 0 and 1). This makes the math neater. The book writes them as $\tilde{y}_n$; this page writes $y_n$.
  • The classifier is a line (a plane, in more dimensions): $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + w_0$. If $f(\mathbf{x}) > 0$, predict $+1$. If $f(\mathbf{x}) < 0$, predict $-1$. The line itself is where $f(\mathbf{x}) = 0$.
  • $\mathbf{w}$ points across the line. It sets the line's tilt. $w_0$ slides the line back and forth without tilting it.
Quick check: what does $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n)$ mean?
It is positive when point $n$ is on the correct side, negative when it's on the wrong side, and bigger the farther it is from the line on the correct side. You will see $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n)$ in almost every equation below, so it's worth remembering: positive = correct, and bigger = safer.

17.3.1Large margin classifiers: draw the widest road

Many lines split the training data perfectly. Pick the one that stays as far as possible from the closest point on each side.

Real-world example: a bank approving loans
A bank has past applicants plotted by yearly income and total debt. Some were approved (blue) and some were denied (orange). The bank wants a line that sorts future applicants. Plenty of lines sort the old applicants perfectly. Some pass very close to an approved applicant and some pass very close to a denied one. A new applicant who is a bit different from the old ones could easily land on the wrong side of those lines. The safest line runs right down the middle of the gap.

The road analogy

Picture the line as the center stripe of a road. Make the road as wide as you can without any point (house) standing on it. The two edges of the road are the curbs, and the distance from the center stripe to a curb is the margin. An SVM picks the line with the largest margin, which is why the book calls it a "large margin classifier."

Why the widest road? New data never looks exactly like the old data. A wide road leaves room for that wobble. If future applicants land a little off from past ones, they are still on the right side.

Turning "widest road" into math, in three steps

Step 1: How far is a point from the line? Plug the point into $f$ and divide by the length of $\mathbf{w}$:

Distance from point to line
$$\text{distance}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert} = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+w_0}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$$

Dividing by $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ turns "how big is $f$" into "how many actual units away is the point."

Step 2: Fix the scale. The equations $2x + 4y - 6 = 0$ and $x + 2y - 3 = 0$ describe the same line. You can multiply $\mathbf{w}$ and $w_0$ by any positive number and the line doesn't move. Since we're free to pick that number, pick it so the closest points have $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$ exactly. The curbs are then the lines $f(\mathbf{x}) = +1$ and $f(\mathbf{x}) = -1$, and by Step 1 the margin is

Margin, after fixing the scale
$$\text{margin} = \frac{1}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$$

Step 3: Make the margin big. Making $1/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ big is the same as making $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ small. The book squares it and puts a $\tfrac12$ in front, which doesn't change the answer and makes the calculus easier. The result is the SVM training problem:

Hard-margin SVM (book eq. 17.75)
$$\min_{\mathbf{w},\,w_0}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_n(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_n + w_0) \ge 1 \ \text{ for every training point } n$$
In plain English
"Make the road as wide as possible ($\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ small), as long as every training point is on its own side and not standing on the road ($y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) \ge 1$)."

This is a quadratic program (a quadratic objective with linear constraints). Standard solvers handle it, and it has a single best answer with no bad local optima. One detail: $w_0$ is not in the objective, because sliding the road sideways doesn't change its width.

Takeaways for 17.3.1

  • Many lines separate the data. The SVM picks the one with the biggest margin (widest road).
  • After fixing the scale so the closest points have $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$, margin $= 1/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, so "maximize margin" becomes "minimize $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$."
  • Every training point must satisfy $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) \ge 1$: on its own side and off the road.

17.3.2The dual problem: only the edge cases matter

Rewrite the problem so that each training point gets a "push" weight $\alpha_n$. When you solve it, almost every point's push is zero. Only the points touching the curbs (the support vectors) hold the road in place.

Real-world example: a fence between two farms
Two farmers want a fence exactly halfway between their fields, as far from both as possible. Where the fence goes depends only on the closest buildings on each side. A barn half a mile back on either farm has no effect. You could move it, tear it down or build ten more barns back there, and the fence would stay put. Those closest buildings "support" the fence's position. In an SVM they are called support vectors.

What "dual" means

Section 17.3.1 solved for $\mathbf{w}$ directly. That is the primal problem. The dual problem asks a different question that has the same answer: how hard does each training point have to push on the road to hold it in place? That push is the number $\alpha_n \ge 0$, one per training point. (Formally, $\alpha_n$ is a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) \ge 1$. "Push" is a good way to picture it.)

Working through the calculus (setting derivatives of the Lagrangian to zero) gives two facts:

What the derivatives tell you (book eqs. 17.78–17.79)
$$\mathbf{w} = \sum_{n=1}^N \alpha_n y_n \mathbf{x}_n \qquad\qquad \sum_{n=1}^N \alpha_n y_n = 0$$
In plain English
Left: the road's direction $\mathbf{w}$ is a weighted mix of the training points, where each point is weighted by how hard it pushes ($\alpha_n$) and which side it's on ($y_n$).
Right: the total push from the $+1$ side equals the total push from the $-1$ side. Otherwise the road would slide over, like a door pushed harder from one side.

Substituting these back in gives the dual problem: find the pushes $\alpha_n$ that

The dual problem (book eq. 17.81)
$$\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\ \sum_n \alpha_n - \frac12\sum_n\sum_m \alpha_n\alpha_m\, y_n y_m\, \underbrace{\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x}_m}_{\text{dot product}} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \alpha_n \ge 0,\ \ \sum_n\alpha_n y_n = 0$$

You don't need to memorize this. One thing to notice now, because 17.3.4 depends on it: the data only appear as dot products $\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x}_m$, which measure how similar two points are. The coordinates themselves never appear.

Why most pushes are zero

At the solution, every point satisfies a condition called complementary slackness (one of the KKT conditions):

Complementary slackness (book eq. 17.84)
$$\alpha_n\,\big(y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) - 1\big) = 0$$

A product is zero only if one of its factors is zero, so every point is in one of two cases:

  • $\alpha_n = 0$: the point doesn't push at all. That's most points: the barns far back from the fence.
  • $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$: the point is exactly on a curb. Only these points can have $\alpha_n > 0$. They are the support vectors.

It's like people leaning on a wall: only those actually touching it can push.

Making predictions with only the support vectors

Put $\mathbf{w} = \sum_n\alpha_n y_n\mathbf{x}_n$ into $f$. Since $\alpha_n = 0$ for everyone else, only the set of support vectors $\mathcal{S}$ remains:

Prediction (book eq. 17.85)
$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}} \alpha_n y_n\, \mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x} + w_0$$

To find $w_0$: each support vector sits on a curb, so $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$, which gives $w_0 = y_n - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_n$. The book averages this over all support vectors to reduce rounding error: $w_0 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}(y_n - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_n)$.

Why anyone cares
1. Compact model: once trained, you can throw away every training point except the support vectors.
2. Size depends on N, not D: the dual has one unknown per training point, not per feature. That helps when there are more features than data points.
3. Only dot products: this sets up the kernel trick in 17.3.4.

Takeaways for 17.3.2

  • The dual gives each point a push $\alpha_n \ge 0$, and $\mathbf{w} = \sum_n\alpha_n y_n\mathbf{x}_n$.
  • Complementary slackness: either $\alpha_n = 0$, or the point is on a curb. The points on the curbs are the support vectors.
  • Predictions use only support vectors, and the data only appear as dot products.

17.3.3Soft margin classifiers: allow a few mistakes

Real data overlaps, so a perfect road usually doesn't exist. Let points cross onto the road, or even past it, but charge a penalty $C$ for every unit of crossing.

Real-world example: a spam filter and Aunt Linda's newsletter
You sort email by number of links and number of ALL-CAPS words. Spam usually has lots of both. Then Aunt Linda sends a family newsletter with 20 links and "HAPPY BIRTHDAY!!!" everywhere. It's real email, but it sits deep in spam territory. The hard-margin SVM from 17.3.1 must get every email right, so it either bends the line badly around this one email or fails completely when no line works. A sensible filter accepts that one oddball is misfiled and keeps a good line for everything else.

Slack variables: how far each point crosses

Give each point a slack $\xi_n \ge 0$ (Greek "xi"), which measures how far it crosses its curb. The constraint becomes $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) \ge 1 - \xi_n$:

  • $\xi_n = 0$: the point is on its own side and off the road. It's behaving.
  • $0 < \xi_n \le 1$: the point is on the road, but still on the correct side of the center line. It's still classified correctly.
  • $\xi_n > 1$: the point is past the center line. It's misclassified.

Each misclassified point has $\xi_n > 1$, so $\sum_n\xi_n$ is always at least the number of training mistakes.

Soft-margin SVM (book eq. 17.87)
$$\min_{\mathbf{w},\,w_0,\,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \underbrace{\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2}_{\text{want a wide road}} + \underbrace{C\sum_{n}\xi_n}_{\text{penalty for crossing}} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_n(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_n+w_0) \ge 1-\xi_n,\ \ \xi_n \ge 0$$
In plain English: $C$ is the size of the fine
Think of $C$ as a fine for each unit a point crosses onto the road.
Big $C$ (strict): crossing is expensive, so the SVM narrows the road to keep points off it. It gets close to the hard margin and can overfit to oddballs like Aunt Linda.
Small $C$ (lenient): crossing is cheap, so the SVM makes the road wide and lets many points onto it. It can underfit.
In practice you choose $C$ by cross-validation.

What changes in the dual: a cap on each push

The dual problem is almost the same as in 17.3.2. The only change is that each push now has a ceiling:

Box constraint (book eq. 17.88)
$$0 \le \alpha_n \le C$$

No single point can push harder than $C$. That's why an outlier can't take over: Aunt Linda's email can push only so hard before the SVM gives up and lets her sit on the wrong side. Each point's $\alpha_n$ tells you where it is:

Push $\alpha_n$Slack $\xi_n$Where the point isSupport vector?
$\alpha_n = 0$$0$Safely on its own side, beyond the curbNo
$0 < \alpha_n < C$$0$Exactly on the curbYes
$\alpha_n = C$$0 < \xi_n \le 1$On the road, but on the correct sideYes
$\alpha_n = C$$\xi_n > 1$On the wrong side (misclassified)Yes

The ν-SVM. $C$ can be hard to interpret, so the book mentions an equivalent setting: $C = 1/(\nu N)$ with $0 < \nu \le 1$. The number $\nu$ ("nu") is easier to read. At most a fraction $\nu$ of the points end up on the road or the wrong side, and at least a fraction $\nu$ of the points are support vectors. For example, $\nu = 0.1$ means "allow up to about 10% of the points to cross."

Takeaways for 17.3.3

  • Slack $\xi_n$ lets a point cross its curb. $\xi_n > 1$ means it's misclassified.
  • The objective trades off a wide road ($\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$) against total crossing ($C\sum\xi_n$). Big $C$ = strict, small $C$ = lenient.
  • In the dual, the only change is $0 \le \alpha_n \le C$. Capping the push keeps any single outlier from taking over.

17.3.4The kernel trick: bend the road

Everything an SVM does uses only dot products $\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x}_m$. Replace each one with a kernel $\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m)$ and the SVM draws a curved boundary, as if the data had first been moved into a much bigger feature space.

Real-world example 1: the thermostat
You record whether people felt comfortable at different room temperatures. Around 66–77°F is comfortable, and both colder and hotter are not. No single cutoff works: "above 66°" also includes 90°. But add a second feature, "how far from 71°, squared", and the problem becomes easy: comfortable means that number is small. In the new two-feature space, a straight line works.

Step 1: Lift the data so a straight line works

This is the general idea. Map each input $\mathbf{x}$ to a longer feature vector $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$, then run the ordinary straight-road SVM on $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$. A straight line in the new space looks curved when you map it back to the original space.

Demo 4a · Slide to add the feature (temperature − 71)² and lift the points

Step 2: The problem is that features get expensive

For a photo with 1,000 pixel values, "all pairs of pixels multiplied together" is about 500,000 features. With all triples it's about 166 million. Some useful feature maps have infinitely many features. Computing $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$ explicitly isn't practical.

Step 3: The trick is that you never need $\boldsymbol\phi$, only dot products

Remember the observation from 17.3.2: training and prediction use the data only through dot products. In feature space those would be $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_n)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_m)$. A kernel is a function that computes that dot product directly from the original inputs, without ever building $\boldsymbol\phi$:

Kernel
$$\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}')$$
Worked example with numbers: the kernel $(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}')^2$ does the work of 3 features

Take $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{x}' = (3, 1)$.

The long way (build features): use $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2)$. Then $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (1,\ 2\sqrt2,\ 4)$ and $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}') = (9,\ 3\sqrt2,\ 1)$. Their dot product is $9 + 12 + 4 = 25$.

The kernel way: $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}' = 1\cdot3 + 2\cdot1 = 5$, and $5^2 = 25$. It's the same answer, with no features built.

For 2 inputs the saving is small. For 1,000 inputs and degree 3, the kernel is still one dot product and one cube, while the feature vector would have millions of entries.

So take the dual from 17.3.2 and replace every $\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x}_m$ with $\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m)$. The prediction becomes:

Kernel SVM prediction (book eq. 17.92)
$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}} \alpha_n y_n\, \mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}) + w_0$$
In plain English: a vote among the support vectors
To classify a new point, compare it with each support vector. $\mathcal{K}$ says how similar they are. Each support vector votes for its own label ($y_n$), and the vote is weighted by its push ($\alpha_n$) and by how similar it is to the new point. Add up the votes. Positive means $+1$ and negative means $-1$.

The offset is found the same way as before, with dot products swapped for kernels: $w_0 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\big(y_n - \sum_{m\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_m y_m\,\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_m, \mathbf{x}_n)\big)$. You never compute $\mathbf{w}$ itself, which is good, since it may have infinitely many entries.

The most popular kernel: RBF (Gaussian)

RBF kernel
$$\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^2\big)$$

It scores 1 for identical points and fades toward 0 as points get farther apart. The setting $\gamma$ ("gamma", which is $1/(2\ell^2)$ in the book's length-scale notation) controls how far each support vector's influence reaches. Small $\gamma$: far, so the boundary is smooth and nearly straight. Large $\gamma$: only a tiny neighbourhood, so each point gets its own little island, which is overfitting.

Takeaways for 17.3.4

  • A straight line in a lifted feature space $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is a curve in the original space.
  • The SVM only needs dot products, so replace $\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{x}_m$ with $\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m)$ and never build $\boldsymbol\phi$.
  • Prediction is a similarity-weighted vote of support vectors: $f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_n y_n\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}) + w_0$.
  • For RBF, $\gamma$ and $C$ together control smooth vs. wiggly. Too big overfits and too small underfits.

One-paragraph summary. An SVM draws the widest road between two groups (17.3.1). Rewriting the problem in terms of how hard each point pushes on the road shows that only the points touching the curbs (the support vectors) matter, and that the data only appear as dot products (17.3.2). Real data overlaps, so you allow points to cross onto the road for a fee $C$, which also caps how hard any single point can push (17.3.3). Finally, replacing every dot product with a kernel lets the same machinery draw curved boundaries, as though the data lived in a huge feature space, without ever building it (17.3.4).
