The story: the apple bin mix-up

Two orchards, one bin, and nobody kept track of whose apple is whose.

Two orchards sell at the same farmers' market. Hillside grows small apples and Valley grows big ones. This morning a helper tipped both crates into a single bin. You weigh all 40 apples, and you would like to know three things about each orchard:

  • its typical weight (a mean $\mu_k$),
  • how consistent its apples are (a spread $\sigma_k$),
  • its share of the bin (a mixing weight $\pi_k$).

You would also like a best guess of which orchard each apple came from. Unfortunately, that is exactly the information that got lost.

The bin. Each dot is an apple, placed at its weight.

Why this is hard: a chicken-and-egg problem

If you knew each apple's orchard…

Estimating each orchard's recipe would be easy. Split the bin into two piles, then average each pile for $\mu_k$, take each pile's spread for $\sigma_k$, and count each pile for $\pi_k$.

If you knew each orchard's recipe…

Guessing each apple's orchard would also be easy. Use Bayes' rule: a 200 g apple is much more likely under Valley's bell curve than under Hillside's.

You know neither. EM breaks the loop by guessing one half, solving for the other, and repeating. Guess the recipes, then softly assign apples to orchards (the E step). Re-estimate the recipes from those soft assignments (the M step). Repeat. The rest of this page shows why that loop always goes uphill, following the same order as the chapter.

an apple's weight$y_n$, the observed data ($n = 1,\dots,N$, with $N=40$)
its orchard$z_n \in \{\text{H},\text{V}\}$, the hidden (latent) variable
the orchards' recipes$\theta = (\pi_k, \mu_k, \sigma_k)$, the parameters we want
how plausible a recipe is$\text{LL}(\theta) = \sum_n \log p(y_n\mid\theta)$, the log likelihood we want to climb
“how much I blame orchard $k$ for apple $n$”$q_n(z_n{=}k)$, which equals the responsibility $r_{nk}$ after an E step
a safety net under the hill$Q(\theta,\theta^t)$, the lower bound (the ELBO) that EM climbs instead

8.7.1The general algorithm: climb a safety net

If a hill is awkward to climb directly, climb a simpler shape that sits under it and touches it where you stand.

Suppose you want to climb a hill $\text{LL}(\theta)$, but the hill has a messy shape. Bound optimization (also called MM, for minorize-maximize) says to build a simpler surface $Q(\theta,\theta^t)$ that obeys two rules, and then jump to the top of $Q$:

The two rules for the surrogate
$$Q(\theta,\theta^t) \le \text{LL}(\theta)\ \text{ for every } \theta \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q(\theta^t,\theta^t) = \text{LL}(\theta^t)$$
The update
$$\theta^{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta\ Q(\theta,\theta^t)$$
$Q \le \text{LL}$
The net lies everywhere under the hill. It minorizes LL.
$Q(\theta^t,\theta^t)=\text{LL}(\theta^t)$
The net touches the hill exactly where you stand now (it is tight).
$\arg\max Q$
Walk to the top of the net, which is easy when $Q$ has a nice shape (here it is a parabola).

Why can't this ever make things worse? Read the chain of inequalities left to right:

$$\text{LL}(\theta^{t+1}) \;\ge\; Q(\theta^{t+1},\theta^t) \;\ge\; Q(\theta^t,\theta^t) \;=\; \text{LL}(\theta^t)$$

The first $\ge$ holds because the net is under the hill. The second holds because $\theta^{t+1}$ is the top of the net, so it is at least as high as any other point on it, including $\theta^t$. The final $=$ is the touching rule. So every step goes uphill (or stays put).

Try it on the apples. Only Valley's mean $\mu_V$ is unknown here; the other recipe numbers are fixed at the truth.

The black curve is the real hill, LL($\mu_V$). The green curve is the net built at your current position (it is the exact bound EM builds, which the next sections derive). Click anywhere on the plot to choose a starting point, then press Step.

What to notice. Far from the peak the net is a poor fit, but it still points uphill. Close to the peak it hugs the hill and the steps shrink, so MM slows down near the top. Here every start reaches the same peak, but MM only promises “never downhill”, not “the highest peak”. When the hill has several peaks, you end up on whichever one you started near (see 8.7.3.5).

8.7.2The EM algorithm: where does the net come from?

EM is MM for models with hidden variables. Its net is built from guesses about the hidden variables.

Each apple's probability under a recipe is a sum over the orchard it might have come from:

$$\text{LL}(\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(y_n\mid\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\Big[\sum_{z_n} p(y_n, z_n\mid\theta)\Big]$$
$p(y_n, z_n\mid\theta)$
“Apple $n$ came from orchard $z_n$ and weighs $y_n$.” For us that is $\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$, a simple expression.
$\log\sum$
The trouble spot. The log sits outside a sum, so it cannot break the expression into simple pieces. Setting the gradient to zero gives no closed form.

If we knew $z_n$, we would have $\log p(y_n, z_n\mid\theta)$ with no sum inside the log, and the problem would be easy (that is the first card in the story). EM's idea is to replace the unknown $z_n$ with a distribution of guesses $q_n(z_n)$ and push the log through the sum, at the cost of an inequality. The next three subsections do exactly that.

8.7.2.1The lower bound: Jensen's inequality

The log of an average is never smaller than the average of the logs.

Pick any blame distribution $q_n$ over the two orchards for each apple. Multiply and divide by it (which changes nothing), then use the fact that $\log$ is concave:

$$\text{LL}(\theta) = \sum_n \log \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\,\frac{p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)}{q_n(z_n)} \;\ge\; \sum_n \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log\frac{p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)}{q_n(z_n)}$$ $$= \sum_n \Big(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_n}\!\big[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\big]}_{\text{expected complete-data log lik.}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{H}(q_n)}_{\text{entropy of the guess}}\Big) \;=\; \mathcal{L}\big(\theta,\{q_n\}\mid\mathcal{D}\big)$$
$\sum_{z} q(z)\,\frac{p(y,z)}{q(z)}$
An average (weighted by $q$) of the values $a_z = p(y,z)/q(z)$. It always equals $p(y)$.
$\log \mathbb{E}[a] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log a]$
Jensen's inequality for the concave $\log$: a chord drawn between two points on a log curve lies under the curve.
$\mathcal{L}(\theta,q)$
The evidence lower bound (ELBO). This is the net. The log now sits inside the sum, on $p(y,z)$, which is the easy quantity.
Jensen's inequality for one apple

Click an apple to select it. The orchards' recipes are the current guess from the E-step demo below, so changing that guess changes this picture too. Slide $q(\text{H})$, how much you blame Hillside for this apple, and watch the gap.

To keep the picture readable, every value is divided by $p(y_n)$. The weighted average of the two points then always lands at $x=1$, where the true $\log p(y_n)$ sits at height $0$. The chord's height at $x=1$ is the ELBO term, and the red gap between them turns out to be exactly $\mathbb{KL}\big(q_n\,\|\,p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\big)$.

What to notice. The gap closes completely at one value of $q$, where both points merge into one. That happens when your blame matches the posterior $p(z\mid y,\theta)$, because then every ratio $a_z = p(y,z)/q(z)$ equals $p(y)$, and averaging identical numbers costs nothing. That observation is the E step.

8.7.2.2The E step: make the net touch

Choose the blame that closes the gap, which turns out to be Bayes' rule.

Rewrite one apple's ELBO term using $p(y,z) = p(z\mid y)\,p(y)$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta,q_n\mid y_n) = \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log\frac{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\,p(y_n\mid\theta)}{q_n(z_n)} = \log p(y_n\mid\theta)\;-\;\mathbb{KL}\big(q_n(z_n)\,\|\,p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\big)$$
$\log p(y_n\mid\theta)$
This apple's true contribution to LL. It does not depend on $q$.
$\mathbb{KL}(q_n\,\|\,\cdot)$
The gap. It is $\ge 0$, and it is $0$ only when $q_n$ equals the posterior.
So the best blame is the posterior
$$q_n^*(z_n) = p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{L}(\theta^t,\{q_n^*\}) = \text{LL}(\theta^t)$$
and that defines EM's net
$$Q(\theta,\theta^t) = \mathcal{L}\big(\theta,\{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\}\big)$$

So the net from 8.7.1 is the ELBO with the blame frozen at what the old recipe $\theta^t$ believed. The touching rule holds because the gap is zero at $\theta^t$. The under-the-hill rule holds because $\text{KL} \ge 0$ everywhere else.

In the story. The E step asks every apple, “given what I currently believe about the orchards, how likely is it that you came from each one?” An apple sitting between the two bell curves gets split blame (a blended color), and that is the right answer, not a failure to decide.

8.7.2.3The M step: climb the net

With the blame frozen, re-estimate each orchard as if the soft assignments were real.

Now hold the blame $q^t$ fixed and maximize the ELBO over $\theta$. The entropy term $\mathbb{H}(q_n)$ does not involve $\theta$, so it drops out and only the first term remains:

$$\ell(\theta) = \sum_n \mathbb{E}_{q_n^t(z_n)}\big[\log p(y_n, z_n\mid\theta)\big], \qquad \theta^{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta\ \ell(\theta)$$
$\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)$
The complete-data log likelihood: easy, because it has no sum inside the log.
$\mathbb{E}_{q^t}[\cdot]$
Average it over the frozen blame. Apple $n$ counts as $r_{nH}$ of an apple toward Hillside and $r_{nV}$ of an apple toward Valley.

For exponential-family models such as Gaussians, this is ordinary maximum likelihood with expected sufficient statistics: weighted counts, weighted sums, and weighted sums of squares. Each orchard's new mean is the balance point of the apples, where each apple weighs as much as the blame it gets.

The M step as a seesaw. Each stick's height is how much that apple is blamed on the orchard.

initialize θ⁰
repeat:
    E step:  q_n(z_n) ← p(z_n | y_n, θᵗ)                       # blame = posterior;  ELBO = LL
    M step:  θᵗ⁺¹ ← argmax_θ Σ_n E_{q_n}[ log p(y_n, z_n | θ) ]  # refit with soft labels
until LL stops increasing

8.7.3Example: EM for a Gaussian mixture model

The same two steps, written out for $K$ Gaussian components in $D$ dimensions.

A GMM says each point is produced by first rolling a $K$-sided die with probabilities $\pi_k$ to choose a component, then drawing from that component's Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$. Our orchards are the case $K=2$, $D=1$.

8.7.3.1 E step: responsibilities

$$r_{nk} = p(z_n{=}k\mid y_n,\theta^t) = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{k'}\pi_{k'}\,\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mu_{k'},\Sigma_{k'})}$$

This is Bayes' rule: the numerator is prior times likelihood, and the denominator normalizes so that each point's responsibilities sum to 1. It is exactly the coloring in the E-step demo.

8.7.3.2 M step: weighted refits

$$\ell(\theta) = \sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\log\pi_k \;+\; \sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\log\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$$

The objective splits into a $\pi$ part and a separate part for each component, so each piece can be maximized on its own:

$$r_k = \sum_n r_{nk}, \qquad \mu_k = \frac{\sum_n r_{nk}\,y_n}{r_k}, \qquad \Sigma_k = \frac{\sum_n r_{nk}\,(y_n-\mu_k)(y_n-\mu_k)^\top}{r_k}, \qquad \pi_k = \frac{r_k}{N}$$
$r_k$
The effective number of points owned by component $k$ (for example, Hillside “owns” 17.3 apples).
$\mu_k$, $\Sigma_k$
The weighted mean and weighted covariance: the seesaw from the M-step demo, generalized to $D$ dimensions.
$\pi_k$
The component's share of the effective count.

8.7.3.4MAP estimation: the one-apple orchard

Maximum likelihood can “win” by cheating: it shrinks one component onto a single point.

Someone dropped a single prize-winning 290 g giant into the bin. Suppose Valley's bell curve parks right on top of it. Then that apple's density is

$$\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mu_k{=}y_n,\ \sigma_k^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_k} \;\xrightarrow{\;\sigma_k\to 0\;}\; \infty$$

The other component can cover the rest of the bin, so LL can be pushed to $+\infty$ by an absurd recipe: “Valley grows apples that weigh exactly 290.000 g.” This singularity is a real failure mode of MLE for GMMs, and it gets worse in higher dimensions.

The fix is to add a prior and do MAP estimation instead. With a conjugate (inverse-Wishart) prior on each covariance and a flat prior on each mean, only the M step changes:

$$\hat\Sigma_k = \frac{\breve{S} + S_k}{\breve\nu + r_k + D + 2}, \qquad S_k = \sum_n r_{nk}(y_n-\bar y_k)(y_n-\bar y_k)^\top$$
$\breve S$
Prior pseudo-scatter: it acts like a few imaginary apples with a sensible spread. A common default is $\breve S = \tfrac{1}{K^{1/D}}\operatorname{diag}(s_1^2,\dots,s_D^2)$ from the pooled data variance, and $\breve\nu = D+2$.
$r_k\to 1$, $S_k\to 0$
Even when a component owns only one apple, $\hat\Sigma_k \ge \breve S/(\breve\nu+D+3) > 0$, so it can no longer collapse.

What to notice. MLE's Valley curve turns into a spike, $\sigma_V$ plunges toward zero, and LL heads to $+\infty$. That looks like a great score, but the model is useless. MAP's Valley still sits on the giant apple, but its spread levels off at a floor set by the prior, so its score stays finite and honest. Lower the prior strength and the floor drops. At strength 0 you would be back to MLE.

8.7.3.5Nonconvexity of the NLL: many hills

The likelihood surface has several peaks, and EM climbs whichever one it starts near.

Fix both spreads at 18 g and both shares at ½, and leave only the two means unknown. The map below shows LL for every pair $(\mu_H, \mu_V)$. There are two equally tall peaks: “Hillside is small, Valley is big” and its mirror image, “Hillside is big, Valley is small”. They describe the same fit with the names swapped. This is label switching. With $K$ components there are $K!$ such copies of every solution, so the negative log likelihood can't be convex.

Click anywhere to start EM there. The path shows each iteration.
stronger color = higher LL ○ start  ● where EM stopped dashed line: $\mu_H=\mu_V$ (the mirror)
What to notice. Every path flows uphill to the nearest peak, so which peak you reach depends only on where you start. A start exactly on the diagonal stays on it, because the two components stay identical forever. It stalls at a saddle point, where both orchards get the same recipe, which is a poor answer. In practice EM is run from several random restarts (or from a smart initialization such as K-means++), and the run with the best final LL is kept.
