The Bias–Variance Tradeoff

A companion to §3.5 & §4.7.6 of Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — built for CS 473 self-study.

1What do “bias” and “variance” actually mean?

Imagine repeating an experiment many times — each time you get a slightly different estimate. Variance asks: how much do your estimates bounce around from one repeat to the next? Bias asks a completely different question: if you averaged infinitely many repeats together, would that average land on the truth, or is it systematically off? A method can have either problem without the other. The classic way to see this is an archer shooting arrows at a target: each arrow is one estimate from one repeat of the experiment.

truth (bullseye) average of all the arrows one arrow (one repeat) typical spread of the arrows bias = truth–to–average gap

2Seeing it in a real prediction problem

You're a data scientist at a rental platform, trying to predict monthly rent from an apartment's square footage. Each month you can only survey a small random sample of n listings — that sample is one "repeat" of the experiment, and it's all you ever actually see. Fit a low-degree polynomial (e.g. a straight line) and it's too rigid to bend with the market's true curve — average its predictions over many different months and you're still off, in the same direction, every time: that persistent gap is bias. Fit a very high-degree polynomial and it happily bends through whichever n apartments happened to get surveyed that month — refit next month on a different sample and the curve changes a lot, even though on average it's closer to right: that swing is variance.

3Why more flexibility isn't always better

Now zoom out: instead of one target apartment size, average the bias and variance across the entire range of square footages, and repeat that for every polynomial degree from 1 to 9. As the model gets more flexible (further right), it can represent the true curve more exactly, so Bias² shrinks toward zero. But with only apartments to learn from, a flexible model also has room to fit sampling noise instead of signal, so Variance grows — and grows explosively once the degree gets close to the sample size. Total expected error (black) is the sum of the two, tracing a U-shape: too simple and bias kills you, too flexible and variance kills you, and the dip in between is the sweet spot.

Because variance can explode across many orders of magnitude, the vertical axis below uses a log scale — each gridline is 10× the one before it — so the interesting low-degree region stays visible instead of being flattened to zero by a few extreme high-degree outliers.
