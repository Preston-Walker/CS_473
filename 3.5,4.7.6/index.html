The Bias–Variance Tradeoff

An interactive companion to §3.5 (sampling distribution of an estimator) and §4.7.6 (the bias–variance tradeoff), Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction
Worked example: predicting monthly rent from an apartment's square footage
The boxed result
$$ \mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta(\mathcal D) - \theta^*)^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta(\mathcal D)] - \theta^*\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\big[\hat\theta(\mathcal D)\big]}_{\text{Variance}} $$
$$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] \;=\; \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2 \text{ (model too rigid)}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\big[\hat f(x_0)\big]}_{\text{Variance (model too sensitive to } \mathcal D \text{)}} $$
The story: you're a data scientist at a rental platform. You want a curve rent = f(sqft) that predicts monthly rent from an apartment's size. Each month you only get to survey a small random sample of n listed apartments — that sample is your training set 𝒟. Fit a low-degree polynomial (e.g. a straight line) and it may be too rigid to capture the true diminishing-returns shape of the market — it misses the same way every month, regardless of which apartments you happened to survey: low variance, high bias. Fit a high-degree polynomial and it happily bends through the noise of whichever n apartments you drew — refit next month on a different sample and you get a wildly different curve: low bias, high variance. The σ slider controls how noisy real rents are around the true curve (measurement noise, unit-to-unit quirks); that noise is irreducible — no model, however flexible, can fit it away.

Built to accompany Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction, §3.5 & §4.7.6 — for CS 473 self-study. The rent/square-footage market is simulated (not real listings): the "true" curve is a fixed, mildly concave function of square footage, and each "month" draws a fresh random sample of noisy apartments from it. Everything here is computed live in the browser via repeated polynomial least-squares fits (Monte Carlo resampling) — no data leaves your machine.
