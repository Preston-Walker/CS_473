FIELD NOTES / machine learning MURPHY · §20.4

MANIFOLD LEARNING

Unrolling
the data

High-dimensional data usually lives on a curved, low-dimensional surface tucked inside the big space. Manifold learning finds that surface and lays it out flat.

Picture a rug rolled up in the corner. Every fibre has three coordinates, but two numbers really describe it: how far along the rug and how far across. PCA (§20.1) can only project the roll, which squashes the layers together. The methods in this section unroll it.

Every figure runs live in your browser on small synthetic datasets. Notation follows Murphy: inputs xn ∈ ℝD, embeddings zn ∈ ℝL with L ≪ D, and N points.

1 · WHAT IS A MANIFOLD? (§20.4.1)

Curved globally, flat up close

A manifold is a space that looks like ordinary flat ℝL if you zoom in far enough, even though it may be curved, rolled or twisted overall. Its intrinsic dimension L is how many numbers you need to say where you are on it. The ambient dimension D is the size of the space it sits in.

L = 2 IN D = 3

The Earth's surface

A city map is flat and works fine; that is "locally Euclidean". But no flat map of the whole globe keeps every distance right. That is why Greenland looks huge on Mercator maps. Two numbers (latitude, longitude) locate you.

L = 1 IN D = 3

A hiking trail

The trail wiggles through 3-D terrain, but "mile marker 4.2" pins down where you are. Hikers measure distance along the trail, not straight through the mountain.

L = 2 IN D = 3

A rolled-up rug

An ant on the rug can walk from the inside to the outside, but only by going around every turn. Through the air, the next layer is close. On the rug, it is far away.

Two lessons that drive the whole section

  • Up close the two distances agree. For beads a short way apart on the same turn, the ratio is ≈ 1. Local Euclidean distances can be trusted.
  • Far apart they can disagree badly. Points on adjacent layers look close in ℝ3 but are far apart on the rug. Any method that trusts long Euclidean distances (like PCA or plain MDS) will fold the layers together.
2 · THE MANIFOLD HYPOTHESIS (§20.4.2)

Real data has far fewer knobs than pixels

The manifold hypothesis says natural high-dimensional data (images, audio, gene expression) concentrates near a low-dimensional manifold. Most of pixel space is static. Murphy illustrates this with a rotating handwritten digit. Below, one 24×24 image is a single point in ℝ576, and rotating it traces a path with only one knob, the angle.

LAB B

576 numbers, one knob

Random pixel vectors are almost always static, so real images fill a vanishingly thin slice of pixel space.

Where this shows up for real

  • Product photos on a turntable: one knob (angle) per product.
  • Face photos: a handful of knobs (head pose, lighting, expression) instead of millions of pixels.
  • Single-cell RNA-seq: ~20,000 genes per cell, but cells follow a few developmental trajectories.
3 · APPROACHES (§20.4.3, TABLE 20.1)

The menu: what does each method try to keep?

Every method here produces one low-dimensional point zn per input xn. They differ in which relationships they preserve. A useful split is global methods, which try to get all pairwise distances right, and local methods, which only care about each point's neighbourhood. A second split is whether the answer comes from an eigenvector problem (a unique, exact solution) or from gradient descent (non-convex, depends on the start).

4 · MULTIDIMENSIONAL SCALING (§20.4.4)

Rebuild a map from a table of distances

MDS gets only an N×N table of dissimilarities dij, no coordinates at all, and must place N points so their distances match. The classic real-world example is a road atlas mileage chart. Hand MDS the distances between cities and it draws the map (up to rotation and reflection, which distances can't reveal).

CLASSICAL MDS · EIGENVECTORS

B = −½ J D(2) J,   Z = UL ΛL½

If the distances really were Euclidean, B would equal the Gram matrix XX⊤ of centred coordinates. So take its top-L eigenvectors, exactly as in PCA. On Euclidean input, classical MDS = PCA. Negative eigenvalues mean the distances can't come from any flat space.

METRIC MDS · STRESS

ℒ = Σi<j (dij − ‖zi − zj‖)²

Match the distances themselves, not inner products. No closed form. The lab uses SMACOF, which repeatedly minimises a simple quadratic upper bound, so stress never goes up.

SAMMON & NON-METRIC

ℒ = Σi<j (dij − ‖zi − zj‖)² / dij

Sammon mapping divides by dij, so small distances count more, which is a step towards "local". Non-metric MDS keeps only the rank order of distances, useful for survey answers like "A is more similar to B than to C".

5 · ISOMAP (§20.4.5)

MDS with road distances instead of crow-flies

Lab A showed that long straight-line distances lie, while short ones are trustworthy. Isomap builds on that. It connects each point to its K nearest neighbours, estimates the geodesic distance between any two points as the shortest path through that graph (like driving on roads), then runs classical MDS on those distances.

THE ALGORITHM
  1. Build a K-nearest-neighbour graph; edge weights are Euclidean distances (trusted because they are short).
  2. Geodesic distances dijG = shortest-path length (Dijkstra or Floyd–Warshall).
  3. Classical MDS on DG: top-L eigenvectors of −½J(DG)(2)J.
THE CATCH
  • Short-circuits. One edge that hops between layers is a tunnel through the rug, and it shrinks many geodesics at once. Raise K or the noise above and watch the embedding fold.
  • Too small K and the graph breaks into pieces with no path between them.
  • Cost: all-pairs shortest paths plus a dense N×N eigenproblem.
  • When the graph is right, it recovers distances along the manifold globally, which makes it great for "unrolling".
6 · KERNEL PCA (§20.4.6)

PCA in a feature space you never build

PCA needs only inner products between centred data points (the Gram matrix). Replace each inner product with a kernel k(xi, xj) = φ(xi)⊤φ(xj) and you get PCA in a (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space φ, without computing φ. In MDS terms it is classical MDS with dij2 = kii + kjj − 2kij.

7 · MAXIMUM VARIANCE UNFOLDING (§20.4.7)

Pull the tangled necklace taut

Take a crumpled necklace and pull its ends. The links between neighbouring beads can't stretch, but everything else moves apart, until the necklace is a straight line. MVU (also called semidefinite embedding) does exactly this. It keeps the distance between every pair of neighbours fixed and maximises the total spread. Spread with locked local distances can only be gained by unfolding.

THE SEMIDEFINITE PROGRAM

maxK tr(K)   s.t.   Kii + Kjj − 2Kij = ‖xi − xj‖² for neighbours,   ΣijKij = 0,   K ⪰ 0

Optimise over the Gram matrix K of the embedding rather than the points themselves. tr(K) is the total variance (spread) of centred points, the constraints lock neighbour distances, and the problem is convex. Then run kernel PCA on the learned K.

TRADE-OFFS
  • Convex, so there are no bad local optima, and the learned kernel fits this manifold.
  • SDP solvers scale badly (roughly N³ or worse), so it is practical only for a few thousand points.
  • Try "Nothing locked" in the lab: without the constraints, maximising variance just inflates the data, so the constraints are what do the unfolding. Lock too few and the result can flex in ways you didn't intend.
8 · LOCALLY LINEAR EMBEDDING (§20.4.8)

Describe each point by its neighbours

Describe your house to a friend without coordinates: "about 40% of the way from the library to the grocery store, nudged towards the park". That recipe still works if someone rotates, moves or resizes the map. LLE writes such a recipe for every point (a weighted average of its neighbours), then finds 2-D positions where the same recipes still hold.

LAB G

Recipes on an S-shaped sheet

Regulariser on the local fit

Recipe for the selected point: xi ≈ Σj wij xj

TWO LEAST-SQUARES PROBLEMS

1.  minW Σi ‖xi − Σj∈nbr(i) wijxj‖²   s.t. Σj wij = 1

2.  minZ Σi ‖zi − Σj wijzj‖² = tr(Z⊤(I−W)⊤(I−W)Z)

Step 1 is a small linear solve per point. Weights summing to 1 make the recipe immune to translation, rotation and scale. Step 2 takes the bottom eigenvectors of the sparse matrix M = (I−W)⊤(I−W), skipping the constant one (eigenvalue 0).

REAL WORLD & GOTCHAS
  • Sensor-network localisation: each sensor only knows its neighbours' relative signal strengths, yet you want a global map. This is the same idea.
  • Sparse eigenproblem, so it is fast and scales to large N.
  • When K exceeds the local dimension, the local fit has many exact solutions, so a regulariser is required. Try 10⁻⁵ vs 10⁻¹ above.
  • Only local structure is kept. Embeddings often look squashed or bunched at the edges, because nothing preserves global scale.
9 · LAPLACIAN EIGENMAPS (§20.4.9)

Rubber bands between neighbours

Seating guests at a long table: connect every pair of friends with a rubber band (stronger for closer friends) and let everyone slide until the bands are as relaxed as possible. Friends end up near each other. To stop the lazy answer (everyone in one chair), require the seats to have a fixed spread. That is Laplacian eigenmaps, and the answer is given by eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian.

THE OBJECTIVE IS A QUADRATIC FORM

½ Σij Wij (zi − zj)² = z⊤Lz,   L = D − W

Wij = exp(−‖xi−xj‖²/2σ²) for neighbours (else 0), and D is the diagonal degree matrix. Minimise subject to z⊤Dz = 1, which gives the generalised eigenproblem Lz = λDz. The constant vector has λ = 0 and is useless, so take the next L.

INTUITION & CONNECTIONS
  • Vibration modes. The eigenvectors are the "harmonics" of the graph, like a guitar string's fundamental and overtones. Eigenvector 1 rises once from end to end, eigenvector 2 waves across or up-and-back, and so on.
  • Same machinery as spectral clustering (§21.5): disconnected clusters give extra zero eigenvalues.
  • Like LLE, it keeps neighbourhoods but not distances, so the layout can be squashed. Higher eigenvectors are often just overtones of the first (look at eigenvector 2 on a long roll), not new directions.
10 · t-SNE AND UMAP (§20.4.10)

Keep the neighbours, let distances go

t-SNE is the method behind most "map of the data" pictures you have seen: single-cell atlases with islands of cell types, word-embedding galaxies, MNIST digit clusters. It turns distances into neighbour probabilities, "if point i picked a neighbour at random, how likely is it to pick j?", in both the original space (pij) and the map (qij). It then moves the map points to make the two match.

LAB I·2

The crowding problem, and why the "t"

In 10 dimensions you can have 11 points all equally far apart. In 2-D you can't. There isn't enough room near each point for all its moderately close neighbours. With a Gaussian on the map side, they all get crushed together in the middle.

t-SNE uses a heavy-tailed Student-t (Cauchy) kernel on the map side. To match a given small similarity, a heavy-tailed kernel needs a larger distance, so moderately dissimilar points are pushed further apart and clusters separate cleanly.

SNE → SYMMETRIC SNE → t-SNE

pj|i ∝ exp(−‖xi−xj‖²/2σi²),   pij = (pj|i+pi|j)/2N

qij ∝ (1 + ‖zi−zj‖²)−1,   ℒ = KL(P‖Q)

Each σi is tuned by binary search so the neighbour distribution has the requested perplexity 2H. Dense regions get small σ, sparse ones large. Minimise by gradient descent with momentum and "early exaggeration" (multiply P by ~4–12 for the first iterations so clusters form first).

READ t-SNE PLOTS CAREFULLY
  • Cluster sizes mean nothing. The per-point σi expands dense clusters and shrinks sparse ones.
  • Gaps between clusters mean little. Global layout is weakly preserved; try "Restart" and watch clusters swap places.
  • Perplexity changes the picture. Very low values make fake little clumps. Always try a few.
  • Non-convex: different seeds give different maps. No f(x) for new points.
COST & UMAP

Exact t-SNE is O(N²) per iteration. The lab does exactly this for 500 points. Real implementations use Barnes–Hut trees or FFT interpolation (O(N log N)) to reach millions of points.

UMAP is the popular successor. It builds a fuzzy K-NN graph, then lays it out with attractive forces along edges and repulsion sampled from non-edges (a cross-entropy loss). It is faster, tends to keep more global structure, and can embed new points. The same reading cautions apply.

12 · SELF-CHECK

Putting it together

ONE IDEA, MANY VARIATIONS
  1. Decide what "similar" means: all distances (MDS), graph distances (Isomap), a kernel (kPCA, MVU) or neighbourhoods (LLE, Laplacian, t-SNE).
  2. Almost all of them use a K-nearest-neighbour graph, because only short distances are trusted on a curved manifold.
  3. Spectral methods reduce to an eigenproblem: top eigenvectors of a Gram-like matrix (MDS, Isomap, kPCA, MVU) or bottom eigenvectors of a Laplacian-like matrix (LLE, Laplacian eigenmaps).
  4. t-SNE and UMAP trade guarantees for pictures, giving the best cluster visualisations through non-convex optimisation.
WHEN TO REACH FOR WHAT
  • Need features for a downstream model or new points later → PCA, kernel PCA, a parametric UMAP or an autoencoder (§20.3).
  • Believe the data is one smooth sheet and want it unrolled faithfully → Isomap (with care over K).
  • Want to see clusters in high-dimensional data → t-SNE or UMAP, with several perplexities and seeds.
  • Never read cluster sizes or between-cluster distances off a t-SNE plot as facts.

Self-check

Why does PCA fail on the swiss roll, even though the roll is only 2-dimensional?

PCA looks for a flat 2-D plane and projects onto it. Any plane through the roll stacks the layers on top of each other, so points far apart on the rug land close together. The roll's 2-D structure is curved, and PCA only knows about flat subspaces.

In Lab C, why do the world cities give a third large eigenvalue and some negative ones, while US cities essentially don't?

Great-circle distances live on a sphere, a 2-D manifold that needs 3 flat dimensions to sit in. Over the whole globe the curvature is large, so a 3rd dimension carries real variance, and the distances aren't exactly Euclidean in any dimension, which shows up as negative eigenvalues. The continental US is a small patch, nearly flat, so 2 dimensions almost suffice.

What goes wrong in Isomap when K is too large, and what goes wrong when it is too small?

Too large: some neighbour edges jump between layers (short-circuits), creating shortcuts that make geodesic distances far too small and fold the embedding. Too small: the graph splits into disconnected pieces, some geodesic distances are infinite, and MDS can't place the pieces relative to each other.

Classical MDS and kernel PCA take top eigenvectors; LLE and Laplacian eigenmaps take bottom ones. Why?

MDS and kPCA eigen-decompose a Gram (similarity) matrix, where large eigenvalues are directions of large spread, which you want to keep. LLE and Laplacian eigenmaps minimise a cost (reconstruction error or rubber-band stretch) written as z⊤Mz, so the best embeddings are the eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues, after discarding the trivial constant vector with eigenvalue 0.

Why do LLE's weights have to sum to one?

So that the recipe is unchanged by translation: adding the same vector c to every point adds Σjwijc = c to the reconstruction, exactly matching xi + c. Rotation and scaling already cancel because the reconstruction is linear. This invariance is what lets LLE reuse the same weights in 2-D.

A colleague's t-SNE plot shows cluster A twice as wide as cluster B, and far from cluster C. What can you conclude?

Very little about sizes and gaps. t-SNE adapts σi per point, which equalises cluster densities, and it only weakly preserves global distances. What you can trust is that points inside a tight cluster were genuinely neighbours in the original space. Check with different perplexities and seeds, or with a method that keeps global structure.

What does perplexity control, and what happens at the extremes in Lab I?

It sets each σi so that point i effectively considers about "perplexity" neighbours. Very low values (2–5) look only at the nearest couple of points and break true clusters into many fake fragments. Very high values approach a global view, blurring nearby clusters together. Typical values are 5–50.
