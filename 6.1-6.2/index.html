Forward vs. Reverse KL: The Mail Carrier and the Food Truck

A companion to §6.2.6 of Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — an interactive version of the bimodal contour figure — built for CS 473 self-study.

1The story: two towns, one van

A valley has two towns, Westfield and Eastport, with a big empty lake between them. Where people live is a two-humped distribution p: dense in each town, almost nobody on the lake. Two businesses each get one van, and the van can only work a single, oval-shaped patch of the valley, a Gaussian q. Each business picks the center, width, and tilt of its oval. The two businesses care about different mistakes, and that is the whole difference between forward and reverse KL.

Minimizes forward KL, KL(p ‖ q)The mail carrier

By law, every resident must get their mail. The carrier's report card is graded at every resident's door: for each person, “how unlikely was the van to be here?” One neglected neighborhood is a disaster, because the penalty grows without limit as q → 0 anywhere people live.

Driving over an empty lake costs nothing, since nobody lives there to complain. So the carrier stretches one big oval over both towns, lake and all.

Minimizes reverse KL, KL(q ‖ p)The food truck

The truck pays for fuel and staff every hour it's parked, and it loses money badly when it parks where nobody is. Its report card is graded wherever the truck goes: “how few customers are there?” Parking over the lake is a disaster, because the penalty grows without limit as p → 0 anywhere the truck works.

Ignoring a whole town costs nothing, since the truck is never graded where it doesn't go. So the truck commits to one town and stays tight around it. Which town it picks depends on where it started.

A guided tour.
  1. Run the mail carrier. The oval swells to cover both towns, and its center lands on the lake, where nobody lives. Residents covered: almost everyone. Van time wasted: a lot.
  2. Drag the van somewhere else and run the mail carrier again. It lands on exactly the same oval. Forward KL has one answer here: it matches the mean and covariance of p (moment matching).
  3. Put the van near Westfield and run the food truck. It shrinks onto Westfield and ignores Eastport entirely. Now drag the van near Eastport and run it again. You get a different answer. These are the two panels on the right of the textbook figure: reverse KL has several local minima.
  4. Turn on the heat map. For the forward integrand, the red sits on whichever town q neglects. For the reverse integrand, the red sits wherever q puts more van time than there are people, which is mostly the lake whenever q spills over it. Even a perfect fit on one town stays faintly red, because the van gives that town all its time while the town holds only half the residents: q/p ≈ 2 there, which is where the ln 2 comes from. Each heat map shows where that business gets penalized.
  5. Try Towns merging. When p is nearly one hump, both businesses agree. The asymmetry only matters when one Gaussian can't fit p well.
  6. Try Big city + small village, then run the food truck from near the village. It can stay stuck on the village even though the city is the better minimum. Gradient descent on reverse KL only finds the nearest valley.

3Why the two directions behave so differently

Both divergences compare the same two distributions, but each takes its average under a different distribution. That choice decides where a mismatch gets punished.

Forward (inclusive) KL — the mail carrier
$$ \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \int p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx $$ Weighted by $p$. Wherever $p(x) > 0$ but $q(x) \to 0$, the log ratio blows up, so $q$ is forced to be non-zero everywhere $p$ is: zero-avoiding, or mass-covering. Where $p \approx 0$ the integrand vanishes, so $q$ can waste mass there for free. For a Gaussian $q$ the minimizer has a closed form, which is the mean and covariance of $p$ (an “M-projection”): $$ \mu_q = \mathbb{E}_p[x], \qquad \Sigma_q = \mathrm{Cov}_p[x]. $$
Reverse (exclusive) KL — the food truck
$$ \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \int q(x)\,\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\,dx $$ Weighted by $q$. Wherever $q(x) > 0$ but $p(x) \to 0$, the log ratio blows up, so $q$ must shut itself off wherever $p$ is tiny: zero-forcing, or mode-seeking. Where $q \approx 0$ the integrand vanishes, so $q$ can ignore whole regions of $p$ for free. There is no closed form, and the objective is non-convex, which is why the answer depends on the starting point (an “I-projection”).

How the page computes the scores: $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p) = -\mathbb{H}(q) - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p]$ uses the closed-form Gaussian entropy plus Gauss–Hermite quadrature under $q$. $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = -\mathbb{H}(p) - \mathbb{E}_p[\log q]$ is closed form in $q$ (since $\log q$ is quadratic), with $\mathbb{H}(p)$ from quadrature. The optimizer runs Adam on $(\mu_x, \mu_y, \log\sigma_x, \log\sigma_y, \operatorname{atanh}\rho)$ with finite-difference gradients.

4Where you'll meet the mail carrier and the food truck in ML

Forward KL in practiceMaximum likelihood hedges

Fitting a model by maximum likelihood is minimizing forward KL from the data distribution, because $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|q_\theta) = \text{const} - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log q_\theta(x)]$. When the model family is too simple, it behaves like the mail carrier: it covers everything and puts mass between the modes.

This is the classic explanation for blurry samples from likelihood-trained image models such as VAEs, and for regression toward a mean answer that no single real example looks like. If half the training photos show a dog facing left and half facing right, a model that can't represent both may produce a ghostly average of the two.

Reverse KL in practiceVariational inference is overconfident

Standard variational inference (maximizing the ELBO, which you'll meet later in the course) minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}))$ against the true posterior. It behaves like the food truck: the fitted posterior locks onto one mode and is too narrow, so it underestimates uncertainty.

A related failure in generative models is mode collapse, where a model produces convincing samples from only a few kinds of output and silently drops the rest. GAN training isn't literally reverse-KL minimization, but it fails in the same mode-seeking way: every sample looks real, and the variety is missing.

The one-sentence takeaway: pick the direction by which mistake is worse for you. If missing real possibilities is unacceptable (coverage, safety, honest uncertainty), you want forward-KL behavior. If generating impossible things is unacceptable (every sample must look real, every parked hour must earn money), you get reverse-KL behavior, along with its blind spots.
