ROC Curves, Read as Bayesian Decisions

A companion to §5.1.3.2 of Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — connecting the utility/cost framework from earlier in the chapter to the ROC curve — built for CS 473 self-study.

1The story: a screening test with a threshold to choose

A blood test produces a continuous risk score for a disease. Patients who actually have the disease tend to score higher, and healthy patients tend to score lower — but the two groups overlap: no score perfectly separates them. A doctor has to turn this continuous score into a binary decision by picking a threshold t: refer the patient for follow-up if their score exceeds t, otherwise clear them. Every choice of t trades off two kinds of mistakes differently. Drag the threshold line below (or the slider) and watch the four possible outcomes shift.

3Bringing back utility: which threshold should the doctor pick?

Both mistakes have a cost, but they are rarely equal. A false negative means a real disease goes untreated until it's worse — expensive and dangerous. A false positive means an unnecessary follow-up procedure — annoying and costly, but far less severe. There's also the disease's prevalence, π: the fraction of screened patients who actually have it. The Bayes-optimal threshold minimizes the expected cost per patient, weighing all three.

Expected cost per patient

What this means

In symbols
$$ \mathrm{EC}(t) = \pi \cdot \mathrm{FNR}(t) \cdot L_{FN} \;+\; (1-\pi) \cdot \mathrm{FPR}(t) \cdot L_{FP} $$
Minimizing $\mathrm{EC}(t)$ means: refer the patient whenever the likelihood ratio exceeds a critical value, $\dfrac{p(\text{score}\mid\text{diseased})}{p(\text{score}\mid\text{healthy})} > K$,   where   $K = \dfrac{(1-\pi)\,L_{FP}}{\pi\,L_{FN}}$.

4The ROC curve: every threshold, all at once

Instead of picking one threshold and reading off one (FPR, TPR) pair, sweep t across every possible value and plot the resulting point at each one. That sweep traces the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve — a picture of the entire menu of tradeoffs the test offers. The dashed diagonal is what a coin flip would achieve (TPR = FPR, no information). Drag the point on the curve itself, or use the threshold slider above — they move together.

Notice what moves and what doesn't: dragging the cost or prevalence sliders in Section 3 slides the optimal point along the curve and tilts the iso-cost line, but the curve's shape never changes — that shape depends only on the test itself (Section 1's “test quality” slider). Costs and prevalence tell you where on the curve to stand; only a better test can push the whole curve up and to the left.
