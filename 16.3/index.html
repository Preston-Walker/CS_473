16.3The idea in one picture

Put a small bump of probability on every data point. Add the bumps up. That's the density.

A Gaussian mixture model (§3.5) estimates a density with $K$ bumps whose locations, widths and weights you have to fit, usually with EM. Kernel density estimation (KDE) gets rid of the fitting by going to the extreme: use one bump per data point, center it on that point, give every bump the same weight $1/N$ and the same width $h$. Nothing is learned except the single number $h$, called the bandwidth.

Because the number of "components" grows with the data, KDE is a nonparametric model, like the KNN classifier in §16.1. Like KNN it is exemplar-based: you keep the whole training set around and do all the work at prediction time.

Two meanings of "kernel". In this section a kernel is a density kernel: a small, symmetric probability density used as a smoothing bump. In Chapter 17 you will meet Mercer kernels $\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$, which measure similarity between two inputs and must give positive-definite Gram matrices. The Gaussian appears in both roles, which is why the names get mixed up, but the requirements are different.

Part 1 · Estimating a density

The building block, the estimator, and the one knob that matters.

16.3.1Density kernels

A kernel is just a small, centered, symmetric bump with area 1.

A density kernel is a function $\mathcal{K}: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_+$ that satisfies

Requirements
$$\int \mathcal{K}(x)\,dx = 1, \qquad \mathcal{K}(-x) = \mathcal{K}(x) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \int x\,\mathcal{K}(x)\,dx = 0$$

So it is itself a probability density, centered at zero. You stretch it with a bandwidth $h > 0$:

Bandwidth-scaled kernel
$$\mathcal{K}_h(x) = \frac{1}{h}\,\mathcal{K}\!\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)$$

The $1/h$ in front keeps the area at 1: a wider bump has to be shorter. Here are the common choices. For the ones with compact support, $\mathbb{I}(|x|\le 1)$ means "zero outside $[-1, 1]$".

"Smooth" means differentiable inside the support, and "smooth at boundary" means it also joins zero smoothly at $\pm1$. Efficiency is how the kernel's best-possible asymptotic error compares to the Epanechnikov kernel's, which is the optimal one under mean squared error. Every entry is above 92%. So the kernel's shape barely matters. Its width matters a lot (see §16.3.3).

Why pick one over another?

  • Gaussian: infinitely smooth, so the estimate is smooth too. But it is never exactly zero, so every data point touches every query. That costs $O(N)$ per evaluation, and you can't skip far-away points.
  • Compact kernels (boxcar, Epanechnikov, tri-cube, triangle): only points within distance $h$ count. With sorted data or a spatial index, evaluation gets much faster. The price is that the estimate can be exactly zero in gaps between data.
  • Boxcar: the KDE is a staircase. It is a "moving histogram" that counts the points in a window (Demo 2).
  • Tri-cube: compact and smooth all the way to its edges. That is why LOESS regression uses it (§16.3.5).

16.3.2The Parzen window density estimator

One kernel per data point, each with weight 1/N.

Given data $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$, the kernel density estimate (also called the Parzen window estimator) is

Kernel density estimate
$$\hat{p}(x \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal{K}_h(x - x_n)$$

Each term is a kernel centered on a data point. Each one has area 1, so dividing by $N$ makes the total area 1. That means $\hat{p}$ is a real density, with no fitting step at all. In $D$ dimensions you use a multivariate kernel. Two standard options:

  • RBF / radial kernel: $\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}) \propto h^{-D}\,\mathcal{K}(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert/h)$, with the same bump in every direction. For the Gaussian this is $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{0}, h^2\mathbf{I})$.
  • Product kernel: $\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} \mathcal{K}_{h_d}(x_d)$, which allows a different bandwidth per feature.

The GMM view

With a Gaussian kernel, $\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac1N\sum_n \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{x}_n, h^2\mathbf{I})$. That is a GMM with $N$ components: one per data point, means fixed at the data, equal weights $\pi_n = 1/N$, and a shared spherical covariance $h^2\mathbf{I}$. You trade EM's local optima and the choice of $K$ for storing every data point and doing $O(ND)$ work per evaluation.

The histogram connection

A histogram also estimates a density: count the points in each bin and divide by $N \times$ (bin width). Its answer depends on where the bin edges fall, though. Shift the grid and the shape changes. A boxcar KDE with bandwidth $h$ is a histogram with a bin of width $2h$ centered on whatever point you query. There is no grid, so no grid artifacts.

16.3.3How to choose the bandwidth

The kernel's shape barely matters. Its width is everything.

The bandwidth $h$ is a classic bias–variance knob:

  • $h$ too small: $\hat p$ is a row of spikes, one per data point. It follows the noise in this particular sample (high variance), and as $h \to 0$ it turns into a sum of delta functions.
  • $h$ too large: real structure, like two separate modes, gets smeared into one blob (high bias). As $h \to \infty$, $\hat p$ becomes one wide kernel.

Rule of thumb

If the true density were Gaussian with std. dev. $\sigma$ and you used a Gaussian kernel, the $h$ that minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error is

Silverman's rule of thumb (1D, Gaussian kernel)
$$h = \left(\frac{4}{3N}\right)^{1/5}\hat\sigma \approx 1.06\,\hat\sigma\,N^{-1/5}, \qquad \hat\sigma = 1.4826\;\mathrm{MAD}(x) = 1.4826\;\mathrm{median}\big(|x - \mathrm{median}(x)|\big)$$

The median absolute deviation (MAD) is a robust stand-in for $\sigma$: the factor 1.4826 makes it match $\sigma$ for Gaussian data, and outliers don't inflate it. In $D$ dimensions you can apply the rule to each dimension separately to get a per-dimension $h_d$ for a product kernel. Note the $N^{-1/5}$: more data means a narrower kernel, but slowly. You need $2^5 = 32\times$ the data to halve $h$.

The rule is only exact when the data really are Gaussian. For multimodal data it oversmooths, because $\hat\sigma$ measures the overall spread, not the width of each mode.

Cross-validation

A data-driven alternative is to pick the $h$ that maximizes the leave-one-out log-likelihood. Each point is scored by a KDE built from all the other points:

Leave-one-out CV for the bandwidth
$$\mathrm{LOO}(h) = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log\!\Big(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j \ne i} \mathcal{K}_h(x_i - x_j)\Big)$$

Leaving the point out is essential. If $x_i$ were included, its own bump would contribute $\mathcal{K}_h(0) = \mathcal{K}(0)/h$, which goes to infinity as $h \to 0$. The likelihood would always pick the spikiest possible estimate. That is the same overfitting problem as a GMM component collapsing onto a single point.

How is Silverman's h rescaled for non-Gaussian kernels?

The asymptotically optimal bandwidth depends on the kernel only through $\delta_\mathcal{K} = \big(R(\mathcal{K}) / \mu_2(\mathcal{K})^2\big)^{1/5}$, where $R(\mathcal{K}) = \int \mathcal{K}^2$ and $\mu_2(\mathcal{K}) = \int x^2\mathcal{K}$. So a bandwidth that is right for the Gaussian converts to kernel $\mathcal{K}$ by multiplying it by $\delta_\mathcal{K}/\delta_{\text{Gauss}}$. That works out to about 1.74× for the boxcar, 2.21× for Epanechnikov, 2.43× for the triangle and 2.61× for the tri-cube. This page computes the integrals numerically. It is the same "canonical bandwidth" idea that makes the bars in this demo comparable.

Things to try. (1) Bimodal, N = 100: Silverman's rule smooths away the dip between the modes, while LOO-CV keeps it. (2) Spike + broad: no single $h$ works. A small $h$ captures the spike but makes the tails wiggly, and a large $h$ flattens the spike. That motivates adaptive bandwidths, like the balloon estimator in §16.3.4. (3) Uniform: every kernel leaks mass past the hard edges. This is boundary bias. (4) Raise N and the best $h$ shrinks, roughly like $N^{-1/5}$. (5) With a compact kernel and tiny $h$, LOO drops to $-\infty$: some point has no other point within $h$.

16.3.2KDE in two dimensions

The same sum of bumps, now as a surface.

Nothing changes in higher dimensions except the shape of the bump. This demo uses product kernels, $\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}) = h^{-2}\,\mathcal{K}(x_1/h)\,\mathcal{K}(x_2/h)$. For the Gaussian, that is identical to the round RBF kernel. For a boxcar it puts a little square on every point, which makes the kernel's footprint easy to see.

One warning from §16.1.2 still applies: in high dimensions, every point is far from every other point. To catch enough neighbors, $h$ must be so large that the estimate loses detail. KDE works best in low dimensions, or on a low-dimensional embedding like the ones in §16.2.

Part 2 · Using the density

A density estimate in each class gives a classifier. A density over $(x, y)$ gives a regression curve.

16.3.4From KDE to KNN classification

KNN is a generative classifier that uses an adaptive-width density estimate.

To classify with densities, use Bayes' rule: estimate a class-conditional density $p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c)$ for each class, multiply by the prior $p(y = c) = N_c/N$, and normalize. There are two ways to estimate those densities locally.

Option 1: fixed window (Parzen)

Fix the bandwidth $h$ and fit a KDE to each class separately. The posterior is the kernel-weighted vote of the training points:

Parzen classifier
$$p(y=c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\frac{N_c}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{n:\,y_n=c}\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n)}{\sum_{c'} \frac{N_{c'}}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{N_{c'}}\sum_{n:\,y_n=c'}\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n)} = \frac{\sum_{n:\,y_n=c}\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n)}{\sum_{n}\mathcal{K}_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n)}$$

The problem: one $h$ can't suit both dense and sparse regions. With a compact kernel, any query that has no training point within $h$ gets $0/0$, and the classifier has no answer.

Option 2: fixed count (the balloon)

Flip it around. Instead of fixing the window and counting points, fix the count $K$ and grow a ball around $\mathbf{x}$ until it holds $K$ points. Call its volume $V(\mathbf{x})$, and let $N_c(\mathbf{x})$ be how many of those $K$ points are in class $c$. The balloon kernel density estimate is then the fraction of class-$c$ data per unit volume:

Balloon density → KNN
$$p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) \approx \frac{N_c(\mathbf{x})}{N_c\,V(\mathbf{x})}, \qquad p(y=c) = \frac{N_c}{N} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p(y = c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\frac{N_c(\mathbf{x})}{N\,V(\mathbf{x})}}{\sum_{c'}\frac{N_{c'}(\mathbf{x})}{N\,V(\mathbf{x})}} = \frac{N_c(\mathbf{x})}{K}$$

The volume and $N$ cancel, and what's left is the KNN classifier from §16.1: the fraction of the $K$ nearest neighbors in each class. So KNN is not just a heuristic. It is a generative classifier that plugs in a nonparametric density estimate with a data-adaptive bandwidth: the ball is small where the data are dense and large where they are sparse. It never returns $0/0$.

16.3.5Kernel regression

Predict y at x by averaging the training y's, weighted by how close their x's are.

Nadaraya–Watson: the conditional mean of a KDE

Fit a KDE to the joint distribution of $(x, y)$ using a product kernel: $\hat p(x, y) = \frac1N\sum_n \mathcal{K}_h(x - x_n)\,\mathcal{K}_h(y - y_n)$. Then compute the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \int y\,\hat p(x,y)\,dy \,/\, \int \hat p(x,y)\,dy$. The $y$-kernel is a density centered at $y_n$, so $\int y\,\mathcal{K}_h(y - y_n)\,dy = y_n$ and $\int \mathcal{K}_h(y - y_n)\,dy = 1$. Everything about $y$'s kernel cancels except its center:

Nadaraya–Watson estimator
$$\hat\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x, \mathcal{D}] = \sum_{n=1}^{N} y_n\,w_n(x), \qquad w_n(x) = \frac{\mathcal{K}_h(x - x_n)}{\sum_{n'=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}_h(x - x_{n'})}$$

The weights are non-negative and sum to 1. So the prediction is a locally weighted average of the training targets. This is kernel regression, also called kernel smoothing. It is "soft" KNN regression: a boxcar kernel gives the plain average of every point within $h$. You can also read off a predictive variance. If the $y$-kernel has variance $\sigma^2$,

Predictive variance
$$\mathbb{V}[y \mid x, \mathcal{D}] = \sigma^2 + \sum_{n} w_n(x)\,y_n^2 - \hat\mu(x)^2$$

That is the kernel's own spread plus the weighted spread of the nearby targets. It is large where nearby $y$'s disagree.

LOESS: fit a local line instead of a local constant

Nadaraya–Watson is the solution of a locally weighted least squares problem with a constant model: $\hat\mu(x) = \arg\min_{\beta_0}\sum_n w_n(x)(y_n - \beta_0)^2$. If you fit a line around each query instead,

Locally weighted (linear) regression
$$(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1) = \arg\min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} \sum_n \mathcal{K}_h(x - x_n)\big(y_n - \beta_0 - \beta_1(x_n - x)\big)^2, \qquad \hat\mu(x) = \hat\beta_0$$

you get LOESS (or LOWESS, "locally weighted scatterplot smoothing"), which classically uses the tri-cube kernel. Every query solves its own small weighted least-squares problem. The payoff is at the edges of the data. Near the left edge, every neighbor lies to the right, so a local average is pulled toward their values and flattens out (boundary bias). A local line follows the trend straight to the edge.

How is this different from a Gaussian process?

Both use a kernel to say "nearby inputs have similar outputs", but they do it differently. Kernel regression averages the training targets with fixed, normalized weights. A Gaussian process (§17.2) uses a Mercer kernel as a covariance and solves a linear system, $\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{y}$. Its weights can be negative, they don't need to sum to 1, and they account for correlations between training points. GPs cost $O(N^3)$ to train, while kernel regression has no training step at all and costs $O(N)$ per query.

One-sentence summary. KDE puts a bump of width $h$ on every training point. Summing the bumps gives a density, voting with them gives a classifier (with an adaptive width, that's KNN), and averaging the targets under them gives a regression. In all three, the bandwidth sets the bias–variance trade-off and the kernel's shape is a detail.
