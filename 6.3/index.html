The big idea, before any equations

You share an apartment with a roommate. Every morning you'd like to guess what they will carry out the door: nothing, a jacket, or an umbrella. Call that $Y$. If you guess blind, you're fairly uncertain. Now you glance out the window and see the weather: sunny, cloudy, or rainy. Call that $X$. Your uncertainty about $Y$ drops.

Mutual information $I(X;Y)$ is the size of that drop, in bits. It answers: “On average, how many yes/no questions does looking at $X$ save me when I try to pin down $Y$?” Every other equation in §6.3 is either a different way to compute that number, or a rule about how it behaves.

You only need two tools from §6.1–6.2:

$H(Y) = -\sum_y p(y)\log p(y)$
Entropy: how uncertain you are about $Y$. It is the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify it. A fair coin has 1 bit and a fair 8-sided die has 3 bits.
$H(Y\mid X) = \sum_x p(x)\,H(Y\mid X{=}x)$
Conditional entropy: how uncertain you still are about $Y$ after seeing $X$, averaged over what you might see.
$\mathbb{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum p\log\frac{p}{q}$
KL divergence: how badly $q$ fails as a stand-in for $p$. It is never negative, and it is $0$ only when $q=p$.

All numbers on this page use $\log_2$, so the units are bits. The book often writes plain $\log$. With natural logs the same quantities come out in nats (1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits), and none of the conclusions change.

6.3.1Definition: distance from independence

MI measures how far the real world is from a world where $X$ and $Y$ ignore each other.

If weather and roommate behavior were unrelated, you could build the joint table just by multiplying the margins: $p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y)$. Call that the “boring” hypothesis. Mutual information is the KL divergence from reality to the boring hypothesis:

Definition
$$I(X;Y) \;\triangleq\; \mathbb{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\big\|\,p(x)\,p(y)\big) \;=\; \sum_{x}\sum_{y} p(x,y)\,\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}$$
$p(x,y)$
How often the pair actually happens. It also weights the average, so common pairs count more.
$p(x)\,p(y)$
How often the pair would happen if the variables were independent.
$\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$
The surprise ratio. If it is $>0$, the pair happens more often than chance (rain and umbrella). If it is $<0$, it happens less often than chance (sun and umbrella). If it is $=0$, the pair is right at chance.
$\mathbb{KL}\ge 0$
So $I(X;Y)\ge 0$, and $I(X;Y)=0$ exactly when $X\perp Y$. Individual cells can be negative, but the weighted total never is.

Pointwise mutual information: the per-cell story

The log inside the sum gets its own name. Rewriting it using $p(x,y)=p(x\mid y)p(y)$ shows what it means:

$$\text{PMI}(x,y) \triangleq \log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = \log\frac{p(x\mid y)}{p(x)} = \log\frac{p(y\mid x)}{p(y)}$$

Read it as: “How much did learning $x$ change my belief in $y$, compared to before?” Mutual information is just the average PMI: $I(X;Y)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\text{PMI}(x,y)]$.

Takeaway. MI is a KL divergence, so all the KL intuition carries over. It measures the cost of pretending $X$ and $Y$ are unrelated when they aren't.

6.3.2Interpretation: uncertainty removed

The same number, rewritten with entropies, becomes “bits saved by looking.”

The KL definition is precise but abstract. Splitting the log turns it into the “before minus after” story from the top of the page:

Key identities
$$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X)$$ $$I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = H(X,Y) - H(X\mid Y) - H(Y\mid X)$$
Show the 3-line derivation
$$\begin{aligned} I(X;Y) &= \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x\mid y)\,p(y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x\mid y)}{p(x)}\\ &= \underbrace{-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x)}_{=\,-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\,=\,H(X)} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x\mid y)}_{=\,-H(X\mid Y)}\\ &= H(X) - H(X\mid Y) \end{aligned}$$

By symmetry, the same argument gives $H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)$. Then substituting the chain rule $H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y)$ gives the other two forms.

$H(Y)$
Questions needed to guess the roommate's gear without looking outside.
$H(Y\mid X)$
Questions still needed after seeing the weather.
$H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)$
Questions saved by looking, which is $I(X;Y)$. Notice that it is symmetric. Weather tells you exactly as much about gear as gear tells you about weather.

6.3.4Conditional mutual information

“How related are $X$ and $Y$ once I already know $Z$?” This can be bigger or smaller than $I(X;Y)$.

Fix the value of a third variable $Z$, measure the MI between $X$ and $Y$ inside that slice of the world, then average over slices:

Definition
$$I(X;Y\mid Z) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{p(z)}\big[\,I(X;Y\mid Z{=}z)\,\big] = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y,z)}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,y\mid z)}{p(x\mid z)\,p(y\mid z)}\right]$$ $$= H(X\mid Z) + H(Y\mid Z) - H(X,Y\mid Z) \;=\; H(X\mid Z) - H(X\mid Y,Z) \;=\; I(Y;X,Z) - I(Y;Z)$$

That last form, rearranged, is the chain rule for mutual information. What a pair of variables tells you about $Y$ is what the first tells you, plus what the second adds on top:

$$I(Y;\,X,Z) = I(Y;Z) + I(Y;X\mid Z)$$ $$\text{and in general:}\qquad I(Z_1,\dots,Z_N;\,X) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} I(Z_n;\,X\mid Z_1,\dots,Z_{n-1})$$
Two stories where conditioning flips the answer

Ignoring $Z$: $I(X;Y)$
Knowing $Z$: $I(X;Y\mid Z)$
Takeaway. A common cause creates dependence that disappears once you condition on the cause, so $I(X;Y\mid Z) < I(X;Y)$. A shared effect can hide dependence that only appears once you condition on it, so $I(X;Y\mid Z) > I(X;Y)$. That is why there is no general inequality between the two.

6.3.5MI as a “generalized correlation coefficient”

For Gaussians, MI and correlation carry the same information. Away from Gaussians, MI still sees dependence that correlation misses.

If $(X,Y)$ is bivariate Gaussian with correlation $\rho$, everything has a closed form:

Jointly Gaussian
$$I(X;Y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\big(1-\rho^2\big)$$
Where it comes from (it uses the identity $I = H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)$)

A Gaussian's (differential) entropy is $\tfrac12\log\big((2\pi e)^D\det\Sigma\big)$. With variances $\sigma^2$ and $\Sigma=\sigma^2\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, we have $\det\Sigma=\sigma^4(1-\rho^2)$, so

$$\begin{aligned} I &= \underbrace{\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)}_{H(X)} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)}_{H(Y)} - \underbrace{\tfrac12\log\big((2\pi e)^2\sigma^4(1-\rho^2)\big)}_{H(X,Y)} = -\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2). \end{aligned}$$

Everything about scale cancels, and only the “squashing” factor $1-\rho^2$ of the joint's area survives.

$\rho = 0$
$I = -\tfrac12\log 1 = 0$. For Gaussians, uncorrelated means independent.
$\rho\to\pm1$
$I\to\infty$. $Y$ becomes an exact (continuous) function of $X$, which would take infinitely many bits to specify.
sign of $\rho$
Doesn't matter, because $\rho^2$ appears. MI measures how much dependence, not which direction.
Drag the correlation $\rho$

So why call MI a generalized correlation? Correlation only detects straight-line dependence. MI detects any dependence. A circle, a parabola, or an X shape can all have $\rho\approx 0$ while $Y$ is tightly determined by $X$. Try the shapes in the MIC demo below and compare the two tiles.

6.3.6Normalized mutual information

Raw MI depends on how much there was to know in the first place. Normalizing puts it on a 0–1 scale.

A perfect copy of a fair coin shares 1 bit. A perfect copy of a fair 8-sided die shares 3 bits. Both are “perfect dependence,” yet the raw numbers differ. The fix comes from the information diagram: the overlap can never be longer than either bar.

Bound and normalization
$$0 \;\le\; I(X;Y) \;\le\; \min\big(H(X),\,H(Y)\big) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{NMI}(X,Y) = \frac{I(X;Y)}{\min\big(H(X),\,H(Y)\big)} \in [0,1]$$
upper bound
$I = H(X) - H(X\mid Y)$ and $H(X\mid Y)\ge 0$ for discrete variables, so $I \le H(X)$. The same argument gives $I\le H(Y)$.
$\text{NMI}=0$
$X\perp Y$.
$\text{NMI}=1$
The less-uncertain variable is completely determined by the other one.

6.3.7Maximal information coefficient (MIC)

For continuous data, chop the plane into a grid, compute discrete MI, normalize, and take the best grid.

For continuous scatter-plot data we don't know $p(x,y)$, and entropy in “bits” is slippery. MIC sidesteps both problems. Lay a $G_x\times G_y$ grid over the points, count points per cell to get a discrete joint table, compute its MI, and divide by the most it could possibly be, $\log\min(G_x,G_y)$. Then search for the grid that makes this ratio largest:

MIC
$$\text{MIC} \triangleq \max_{G:\;G_x G_y \,\le\, B(N)} \;\frac{I\big((X,Y)\big|_G\big)}{\log\min(G_x,\,G_y)}, \qquad B(N)\approx N^{0.6}$$
$(X,Y)|_G$
The data discretized by grid $G$: each point becomes a (column, row) pair.
$\log\min(G_x,G_y)$
The largest MI a grid with that many rows and columns could show, so the ratio is like NMI and lands in $[0,1]$.
$G_xG_y\le B(N)$
Caps the number of cells. Without the cap, a grid with one point per cell would make anything look perfectly dependent.
characteristic matrix
$M(G_x,G_y)$ is the best normalized score for each grid shape. MIC is the largest entry.

That makes these values a slight underestimate. Small samples also give MI a small upward bias, which is why “No relationship” doesn't hit exactly 0.

Takeaway. On the circle or X shape, $\rho\approx 0$ but MIC is clearly high. That is the “generalized correlation” claim of 6.3.5 made concrete, and MIC is a practical way to rank many variable pairs by dependence strength.

6.3.8The data processing inequality

You can't create information about $X$ by processing data. Every step can only keep it or lose it.

Think of the telephone game. A message $X$ is whispered to person $Y$, who whispers it to $Z$. $Z$ only hears $Y$, never $X$ directly, so $X\to Y\to Z$ is a Markov chain. However cleverly $Z$ processes what they heard, they can't know more about $X$ than $Y$ did:

Data processing inequality
$$X \to Y \to Z \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I(X;Y) \;\ge\; I(X;Z)$$
Proof: the chain rule, applied in two orders
$$I(X;\,Y,Z) \;=\; I(X;Z) + I(X;Y\mid Z) \;=\; I(X;Y) + \underbrace{I(X;Z\mid Y)}_{=\,0\text{ (Markov)}}$$

Given $Y$, $Z$ adds nothing about $X$, so the last term is 0. Therefore $I(X;Y)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y\mid Z)\ge I(X;Z)$, because MI is never negative. Equality needs $I(X;Y\mid Z)=0$, which means $Z$ kept everything $Y$ knew about $X$.

A telephone game with a 4-letter message (A, B, C, D, each equally likely: $H(X)=2$ bits)

“Mishears” means the letter is replaced by a uniformly random letter, so it may land on the right one by luck. The bars can stay level or step down but never step up. Relabeling is invertible, so it keeps everything (equality). Merging throws information away.

Why ML cares. Every feature extractor, preprocessing step, or network layer is a $Z=f(Y)$. It can make information easier to use, but it can't add information about the label that wasn't in the raw input.

6.3.9Sufficient statistics

A statistic is sufficient when compressing the data with it loses nothing about the parameter, so the DPI holds with equality.

Apply the DPI with the parameter $\theta$ as the source. The world picks $\theta$, the data $\mathcal D$ is generated from it, and then you summarize the data with some statistic $s(\mathcal D)$:

DPI for statistics
$$\theta \to \mathcal{D} \to s(\mathcal{D}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I\big(\theta;\,s(\mathcal D)\big) \;\le\; I(\theta;\,\mathcal D)$$ $$s \text{ is \textbf{sufficient} for } \theta \iff I\big(\theta;\,s(\mathcal D)\big) = I(\theta;\,\mathcal D)$$
equality
Equivalently, $\theta\to s(\mathcal D)\to\mathcal D$ is also a Markov chain. Once you know $s(\mathcal D)$, the rest of the raw data tells you nothing more about $\theta$.
minimal sufficient
A sufficient statistic that is a function of every other sufficient statistic. It compresses the data as far as possible without losing anything about $\theta$. The full dataset is always sufficient, just not minimal.
Coin with unknown bias $\theta$: which summaries of $n$ flips are sufficient?

$\theta$ is drawn uniformly from 19 values $\{0.05, 0.10, \dots, 0.95\}$. All $2^n$ flip sequences are enumerated, so these are exact MI values, not estimates. The dashed line is $I(\theta;\mathcal D)$, the most any summary could keep.

Takeaway. “Number of heads” reaches the dashed line exactly, at every $n$, while being an enormous compression ($n+1$ possible values instead of $2^n$). It is the minimal sufficient statistic for a Bernoulli parameter. That is why the likelihood $\theta^{N_1}(1-\theta)^{N_0}$ only depends on the counts.

6.3.10Fano's inequality

If features $X$ leave a lot of uncertainty about the label $Y$, no classifier can be accurate. MI turns that into a hard floor on error.

Let $\hat Y=f(X)$ be any classifier's guess and let $E$ be the event that it is wrong, with $P_e=P(\hat Y\ne Y)$. Since $Y\to X\to\hat Y$ is a Markov chain, Fano's inequality says:

Fano's inequality
$$H(Y\mid X) \;\le\; H(Y\mid \hat Y) \;\le\; H(E) + P_e\log|\mathcal Y|$$ $$\Longrightarrow\qquad P_e \;\ge\; \frac{H(Y\mid X) - 1}{\log_2|\mathcal Y|} \qquad(\text{using } H(E)\le 1 \text{ bit})$$
$H(Y\mid X)\le H(Y\mid\hat Y)$
This is the DPI in entropy form. The guess is computed from $X$, so it can't leave you less uncertain than $X$ itself does.
$H(E)$
Recovering $Y$ from $\hat Y$ takes one yes/no question first: “was the guess right?” That costs at most 1 bit.
$P_e\log|\mathcal Y|$
If it was wrong (probability $P_e$), you still have to say which class it really was. That costs at most $\log|\mathcal Y|$ bits.
the floor
Since $H(Y\mid X) = H(Y) - I(X;Y)$, choosing features with higher MI with the label lowers the floor on error. This is the theoretical case for MI-based feature selection.

A slightly sharper version replaces $\log|\mathcal Y|$ with $\log(|\mathcal Y|-1)$ and keeps $H(E)$ exactly: $H(Y\mid X)\le H_b(P_e)+P_e\log(|\mathcal Y|-1)$. Both are drawn below.
