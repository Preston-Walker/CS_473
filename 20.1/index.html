PRINCIPAL COMPONENTS ANALYSIS

Finding the
best shadow

PCA squashes data onto fewer dimensions by projecting it onto a few well-chosen directions. The directions it picks are the ones where the data are most spread out.

Imagine shining a light on a cloud of points and looking at its shadow on a line. Most lines give you a cramped shadow where points pile on top of each other. One line gives the widest shadow, and that one keeps the most information. That line is the first principal component.

Every figure on this page runs in your browser on small synthetic datasets. Drag, slide and step through them. Notation follows Murphy: data xn ∈ ℝD, codes zn ∈ ℝL, projection matrix W (D×L).

1 · THE IDEA

Compress, then rebuild

PCA is a pair of very simple maps. An encoder turns each D-dimensional point into L numbers (L < D), and a decoder turns those L numbers back into a point in the original space. Both are just multiplications by the same matrix W, whose L columns are unit-length and at right angles to each other.

ENCODE

Project

zn = W⊤(xn − μ)

Each code znj is the dot product of the centred point with direction wj: how far along that direction the point sits. It is the point's shadow.

DECODE

Reconstruct

x̂n = μ + W zn

Walk zn1 steps along w1, zn2 along w2, and so on, starting at the mean. You land on the closest point of the line, plane or subspace.

SCORE

Reconstruction error

ℒ(W) = 1N Σn ‖xn − x̂n‖²

PCA chooses the W that makes this as small as possible. The rest of the page is about what that W turns out to be and how to find it.

WHY THE TWO GOALS ARE THE SAME · PYTHAGORAS

Each orange drop-line meets the green line at a right angle. So for every point, the squared distance from the mean splits into two pieces:

‖xn − μ‖² = zn² + ‖xn − x̂n‖²

Averaging over points: total variance = variance kept + reconstruction error. The left side doesn't depend on the line at all. So minimising the reconstruction error and maximising the variance of the projections are the same problem, which is why the two curves in the chart are mirror images. Murphy's derivation (§20.1.2) reaches the same fact algebraically: once you plug in the best codes zn1 = w1⊤xn, the loss becomes a constant minus w1⊤Σ̂w1.

3 · WHY THE ANSWER IS AN EIGENVECTOR

The covariance matrix already knows the answer

Sweeping every angle works in 2-D, but not with 400 dimensions. We need to find the best direction directly. The key fact is that the variance of the data along any unit direction v is a quadratic form in the empirical covariance matrix Σ̂:

variance along v = 1N Σn (v⊤(xn − μ))² = v⊤Σ̂v

So the problem is: maximise v⊤Σ̂v subject to ‖v‖ = 1. Drag the arrow v below and look at where the matrix sends it.

What to notice

  • In general, Σ̂ turns v towards the long axis of the cloud and stretches it.
  • In exactly two directions, it doesn't turn v at all, only stretches it: Σ̂v = λv. Those are the eigenvectors.
  • Along an eigenvector the variance is v⊤Σ̂v = λ. The bigger λ is w1; the smaller is w2.
  • Pressing “multiply” over and over (power iteration) always swings v onto w1, because the stretch along it is the largest.
MURPHY §20.1.2 · THE LAGRANGIAN STEP

Add a Lagrange multiplier λ1 for the unit-length constraint and set the gradient to zero:

∂/∂w1 [ w1⊤Σ̂w1 + λ1(1 − w1⊤w1) ] = 2Σ̂w1 − 2λ1w1 = 0  ⟹  Σ̂w1 = λ1w1

Any stationary direction is an eigenvector, and its variance is w1⊤Σ̂w1 = λ1. To make the variance largest, choose the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. Repeating the argument for a second direction that must be orthogonal to the first gives the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue, and so on. With L directions kept, the reconstruction error is exactly the sum of the eigenvalues you threw away: ℒ = Σj>L λj.

4 · THE RECIPE

Centre, measure, rotate, drop

Put together, PCA is four short steps. Here they are on 60 flowers, one stage at a time. Step through and watch where each point goes.

LAB C

PCA one step at a time

    –

    IN CODE · EIGENDECOMPOSITION 
    Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)          # 1. centre
S  = Xc.T @ Xc / N               # 2. covariance (D×D)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)       # 3. eigenpairs
order  = np.argsort(lam)[::-1]
W  = V[:, order[:L]]             #    top-L directions
Z  = Xc @ W                      # 4. codes (N×L)
Xhat = Z @ W.T + X.mean(axis=0)  #    reconstruction
    IN CODE · SVD (MURPHY §20.1.3.3) 
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
W   = Vt[:L].T                   # same directions
lam = s**2 / N                   # same eigenvalues
Z   = U[:, :L] * s[:L]           # same codes

    Libraries use the SVD of the centred data instead of forming Σ̂. It is more numerically stable and gives the same W. When N < D (few images, many pixels), the same trick lets you work with the N×N matrix XX⊤ instead of the huge D×D one.

    7 · CHOOSING THE NUMBER OF DIMENSIONS (§20.1.4)

    How many components should you keep?

    Each eigenvalue λj is the variance along its direction, and the error with L components is the sum of the eigenvalues left out. So the eigenvalue spectrum tells you directly what each extra component buys. These charts use the same 400-pixel faces; the slider is shared with Lab E.

    LAB F

    –

    A subtlety Murphy points out: the held-out error never goes back up. Adding a direction can only bring the subspace closer to any point, seen or unseen, so reconstruction error can't detect overfitting the way test error does for a classifier. That's why people fall back on the scree elbow, a variance-explained threshold, or the profile likelihood method (fit one simple model to the large eigenvalues and another to the small ones, and pick the split L that makes the two-model fit most likely). A probabilistic version of PCA (§20.2) gives a proper likelihood instead.

    8 · UNITS MATTER (§20.1.3.1)

    Standardise first, or the biggest numbers win

    PCA maximises variance, and variance depends on units. Measure height in millimetres and its variance is 100× larger than in centimetres, so the first component will chase height. The usual fix is to standardise each feature to mean 0 and variance 1 first. That is the same as running PCA on the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix.

    9 · SELF-CHECK

    Putting it together

    THE WHOLE ALGORITHM ON ONE CARD
    1. Centre the data: subtract μ. (Standardise too if features have different units.)
    2. Form Σ̂ = 1NXc⊤Xc, or take the SVD of Xc.
    3. The principal directions w1, w2, … are its eigenvectors, sorted by eigenvalue λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ ….
    4. Keep the top L as the columns of W; codes are z = W⊤(x − μ).
    5. Variance kept = λ1+…+λL; reconstruction error = λL+1+…+λD.
    WHAT PCA CAN'T DO
    • It only finds flat subspaces. Data on a curved surface (a spiral, a ring) needs nonlinear methods such as autoencoders or manifold learning (§20.3–20.4).
    • Variance isn't the same as importance. A low-variance direction can be exactly the one that separates two classes.
    • It is sensitive to scaling (Lab G) and to outliers, which inflate variance in their direction.
    • But it is fast, has no hyperparameters except L, has a closed-form answer, and is an excellent first look at almost any dataset.

    Self-check

    Why must the data be centred before computing the directions?

    The directions are meant to pass through the middle of the cloud. Without centring, X⊤X/N measures spread around the origin, so the first “component” mostly points from the origin to the mean, which says nothing about how the data vary.

    In Lab A, the best line kept about 93% of the variance. What is the reconstruction error in terms of eigenvalues?

    It is λ2, the eigenvalue of the discarded direction. Kept variance is λ1, and λ1 + λ2 is the total variance (the trace of Σ̂).

    Why are the principal directions orthogonal to each other?

    Because Σ̂ is symmetric, its eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. It also follows from the derivation: each new direction is chosen to maximise variance subject to being orthogonal to the previous ones. As a bonus the codes z1, z2, … are uncorrelated.

    If every eigenvalue of Σ̂ were the same, what would PCA do?

    The cloud would be a round ball, equally spread in every direction, so no direction is better than another. Every unit vector is an eigenvector, the answer isn't unique, and keeping L of D components keeps exactly L/D of the variance. PCA only helps when the spectrum falls off.

    Your features are income in dollars and age in years. You run PCA and PC1 is almost entirely income. Is income the most important feature?

    Not necessarily. Dollar amounts have a variance in the millions while ages vary by tens, so PCA on the covariance matrix just chases the bigger numbers. Standardise first (use the correlation matrix) and see whether the answer changes.

    Why doesn't held-out reconstruction error rise as L grows, unlike test error for a classifier?

    A bigger L means a bigger subspace that contains the smaller one, so every point, training or not, can only get closer to it. In the limit L = D, every point is reconstructed perfectly. Reconstruction error can't tell signal from noise, so L has to be chosen another way (scree elbow, variance threshold, profile likelihood, or a probabilistic model).
